Calling and Contact Signals

Signal Morse Common Meaning Play
CQ -.-. --.- Calling any station. Used to initiate contact with any amateur radio operator who may be listening. "CQ DX" specifically calls distant stations.
DE -.. . "From" or "This is". Used to identify the sending station. Example: "CQ CQ DE W1ABC" means "Calling any station, this is W1ABC".
K -.- Invitation to transmit. Indicates the sending station is finished and expects a response. Used to hand over the conversation.

Message Control Signals

Signal Morse Common Meaning Play
AR .-.-. End of message. Indicates the sending station has finished their transmission and is ending the current message. Often used as "AR" or "+".
SK ...-.- End of contact. Indicates the sending station is completely ending the conversation and signing off. Also written as "SK".
BT -...- Break. Used to separate parts of a message or indicate a pause within a transmission. Similar to a paragraph break in written text.
AA .- .- End of line. Used to indicate the end of a line of text in formatted messages, particularly in formal communications.

Acknowledgment and Response Signals

Signal Morse Common Meaning Play
R .-. Received / Roger. Acknowledges that a message has been received and understood. "Roger" is the phonetic equivalent used in voice communications.
QSL --.- ... .-.. Confirmation or acknowledgment. Used to confirm receipt of a message or to confirm a contact (QSO) in amateur radio logging.
QRZ --.- .-. --.. "Who is calling me?" Used when a station hears their call sign but didn't catch the calling station's identification.
QRM --.- .-. -- Interference. Used to report that there is interference from other stations affecting the communication.
QRN --.- .-. -. Static/atmospheric noise. Used to report that atmospheric noise is affecting reception quality.

Operating and Status Signals

Signal Morse Common Meaning Play
QTH --.- - .... Location. Used to ask for or provide a station's location. Example: "QTH New York" means "My location is New York".
QSY --.- ... -.-- Changing frequency. Used to indicate that a station is moving to a different frequency. Example: "QSY 7.025" means "Moving to 7.025 MHz".
QRL --.- .-. .-.. "Are you busy?" Used to ask if a frequency is in use before transmitting. A common courtesy in amateur radio.
QRM --.- .-. -- Interference. Used to report interference from other stations.
QRP --.- .-. .--. Low power operation. Used to indicate or ask about low-power transmissions. "QRP" typically means 5 watts or less.

Understanding Signal Context

Signals Are Context-Dependent

The meaning of Morse signals can vary depending on the communication context. While the signals listed above have common meanings, their exact interpretation may differ based on:

  • Communication Type: Maritime, aviation, amateur radio, military, or emergency services
  • Regional Practices: Different regions may have slight variations in signal usage
  • Specific Protocols: Certain organizations or operations may use modified signal sets
  • Modern Adaptations: Digital modes and new technologies may incorporate traditional signals differently

Q-Codes

Many signals starting with "Q" are Q-codes, standardized three-letter codes used in radio communications. They were originally developed to simplify communication and reduce transmission time. Each Q-code has a question form and an answer form.

Procedural Signals

Procedural signals (prosigns) like AR, SK, and BT are unique combinations that represent common phrases. They're sent as continuous characters without the usual inter-character spacing, making them faster to transmit than spelling out the words.

Learning Signals

Start with Essentials

Begin with the most critical signals: SOS (emergency), CQ (calling), K (over), and R (received). These form the foundation of Morse communication.

Practice in Context

Signals are most useful when understood in context. Practice recognizing them within simulated conversations rather than in isolation.

Audio Recognition

Use the play buttons above to hear each signal. Focus on the rhythm and pattern - signals often have distinctive audio characteristics that help with quick recognition.

Real-World Application

While Morse code is less common in everyday communication today, these signals remain important in amateur radio, emergency communications, and as part of radio operator training.
